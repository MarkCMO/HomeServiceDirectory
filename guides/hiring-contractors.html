How to Hire a Trustworthy Home Service Contractor

Verify licenses, check insurance, compare bids, read the contract, and avoid the scams that cost homeowners billions every year.

Updated April 2026 12 min read By HomeServiceDirectory Editorial Team

Hiring the wrong contractor is one of the most expensive mistakes a homeowner can make. The Federal Trade Commission estimates Americans lose over $3 billion annually to contractor fraud and poor workmanship. This guide gives you the specific steps to find, vet, and hire right -- every time.

Where to Find Qualified Contractors

Not all sources are equal. Here's a ranking of sourcing methods from most to least reliable:

Best sources

  • Verified directories like HomeServiceDirectory: Providers must submit credentials and are scored on licensing, insurance, reviews, and profile completeness. Leads are exclusive -- no competing for your project against 5 other contractors.
  • Personal referrals from neighbors/friends: Someone who recently had similar work done and was satisfied is a strong signal. Ask specifically about communication, timeliness, and final cost vs. estimate.
  • State licensing board approved contractor lists: Some states publish lists of licensed, bonded, and insured contractors by trade and county.

Use with caution

  • General search engine results: Rankings don't correlate with quality or licensing. Anyone can buy ads.
  • Nextdoor / neighborhood apps: Useful for initial names, but no vetting of licensing or insurance.
  • Craigslist / Facebook Marketplace: Higher prevalence of unlicensed operators. Use only as a last resort and verify everything manually.
Never hire a door-to-door contractor, especially after a storm. "Storm chasers" target neighborhoods after weather events, offer cheap quick repairs, collect deposits, and disappear. Legitimate contractors have established local businesses and don't solicit door-to-door.

Step 1 -- Verify the License

Licensing requirements vary by state and trade, but most major home service categories require state or local licensing. An unlicensed contractor is a massive liability -- for them AND for you.

Why this matters

  • Unlicensed work may not pass inspection or be covered by homeowners insurance
  • You may be liable for injuries to unlicensed workers on your property
  • You have less legal recourse if work is defective
  • Some states make hiring unlicensed contractors a violation that voids your permit

How to verify: Search "[your state] contractor license lookup" online. Enter the contractor's name, company name, or the license number they provided. Confirm the license is: (1) active/current, (2) covers the work type, (3) in the right jurisdiction.

Step 2 -- Verify Insurance

Every contractor doing work on your property should carry at minimum two types of insurance:

General Liability Insurance

Covers damage the contractor causes to your property (accidentally cuts through a wall, breaks a window, floods a room). Minimum: $1 million per occurrence. Look for $2 million aggregate coverage for larger projects.

Workers' Compensation Insurance

If a worker is injured on your property and the contractor doesn't have workers' comp, you could be liable for their medical bills and lost wages. This is the most overlooked risk in contractor hiring.

How to verify insurance

  • Ask for a Certificate of Insurance (COI) -- a standard document listing policy numbers, coverage amounts, and expiration dates
  • Call the insurer directly (number on the COI) to confirm the policy is active
  • For large projects, ask to be added as an Additional Insured on their GL policy
A contractor who hesitates to provide a COI or gets defensive about insurance verification is a major red flag. Legitimate professionals provide this instantly and understand why you need it.

Step 3 -- Get Multiple Written Bids

For any job over $1,000, get at least 3 written estimates. Here's why:

  • Establishes a market-rate baseline for the work
  • Reveals wildly low bids (often meaning cut corners, inferior materials, or a bait-and-switch)
  • Reveals wildly high bids (often meaning overcharging an uninformed homeowner)
  • Forces contractors to be specific about materials and scope -- vague estimates lead to disputes

What to look for in an estimate

Detailed scope of workEvery task listed, not just a summary
Materials specifiedBrand, grade, quantity -- not just "shingles" or "pipe"
Labor costs broken outNot bundled into one line item
Permit costsWho pulls permits and who pays for them
TimelineStart date, milestones, completion estimate
WarrantyBoth parts (materials) and labor warranty periods
Choosing by price alone is a mistake. The lowest bid is often lowest for a reason. Prioritize: verified licensing + insurance + reviews + communication + written estimate quality. Price is one factor, not the only factor.

Step 4 -- Research Reviews and References

Where to look

  • Google Business Profile: Largest volume, hard to manipulate at scale, shows patterns over time
  • HomeServiceDirectory reviews: Verified project reviews from real customers
  • BBB (Better Business Bureau): Complaint history and resolution record
  • Your state contractor board: Check for disciplinary actions, complaints, or license suspensions

How to evaluate reviews

  • Look for patterns, not just the score -- a 4.2 with 200 reviews beats a 5.0 with 3 reviews
  • Read negative reviews and how the contractor responded
  • Look for specifics -- vague generic reviews may be fake
  • Ask for 2-3 direct references (past customers) for large projects and actually call them

Step 5 -- Require a Written Contract

Never start work without a signed written contract. Verbal agreements are unenforceable for most home improvement work and leave you with no recourse if disputes arise.

Every contract must include:

  • Full contractor name, business name, license number, address
  • Detailed scope of work (what IS included -- and what is NOT)
  • Materials specifications (brand, model, grade, quantity)
  • Start date and estimated completion date
  • Payment schedule tied to milestones (not time)
  • Change order process (how scope changes are priced and approved)
  • Who is responsible for pulling permits
  • Warranty terms for labor and materials
  • Cleanup and disposal responsibilities
  • Dispute resolution process
  • Termination conditions for both parties

Step 6 -- Negotiate Fair Payment Terms

Industry-standard payment structure

  • Deposit at signing: 10-30% (enough to cover initial materials purchase)
  • Progress payments: Tied to specific milestones (e.g., framing complete, rough plumbing complete)
  • Final payment: 10-15% held until work is complete, inspected, and all punch list items resolved

Red flags in payment requests

  • Requesting full payment upfront before any work begins
  • Accepting only cash (and offering a big "cash discount")
  • Requesting payment to a personal name rather than business account
  • Pressure to pay before you've inspected completed milestones
  • No receipt or documentation for payments made
Pay by check or credit card -- never cash for large payments. Credit cards give you chargeback protection if the contractor fails to complete work. Keep all receipts.

Permits and Inspections

Most significant home improvement work requires a permit from your local building department. This is not optional -- and it's not just bureaucracy.

Why permits matter

  • Permitted work is inspected by a licensed building inspector -- an independent check on quality
  • Unpermitted work can cause problems when you sell your home (must be disclosed)
  • Insurance may not cover damage from unpermitted work
  • You could be required to tear out unpermitted work at your expense

Most licensed contractors will pull permits as part of the project. Confirm this is in your contract. If a contractor says "you don't need a permit for this" when you know you do, consider it a red flag.

Complete Red Flag List

Walk away if a contractor:

  • Cannot provide a current license number (or license doesn't check out)
  • Refuses to provide a Certificate of Insurance
  • Demands full payment upfront
  • Only accepts cash
  • Solicited your business door-to-door (especially post-storm)
  • Pressures you to decide immediately or "lose the deal"
  • Cannot provide local references from recent similar projects
  • Gives a quote without seeing the work in person
  • Quote is dramatically lower than all others (by 40%+)
  • Won't provide a written contract
  • Suggests skipping permits to "save money"
  • Has no physical business address
  • Uses high-pressure tactics or scare stories about what will happen if you don't hire immediately

Signs of a trustworthy contractor:

  • Readily provides license number and COI without being asked
  • Has an established local business with physical address
  • Provides detailed written estimates itemizing labor and materials
  • Explains the work clearly in plain language
  • Provides references from similar local projects
  • Pulls all required permits
  • Has consistent positive reviews over multiple years
  • Communicates proactively throughout the project
  • Stands behind their work with a written warranty

During the Project

  • Be available for questions but give the crew space to work
  • Document all change orders in writing before work proceeds
  • Do a walkthrough at each milestone before releasing progress payments
  • Note any concerns immediately -- don't wait until the end
  • Be present for the building inspector's visit if possible
  • Keep copies of all contracts, receipts, permits, and inspection reports

Final Walkthrough and Completion

  • Do a detailed final walkthrough before releasing final payment
  • Create a written punch list of any incomplete or unsatisfactory items
  • Confirm all permits are closed and a final inspection passed
  • Get copies of any manufacturer warranties for materials installed
  • Get operation manuals for any new equipment (HVAC, water heater, etc.)
  • Release final payment only when the punch list is complete

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I verify a contractor's license?

Search "[your state] contractor license lookup" to find your state's licensing board portal. Enter the contractor's name, company name, or license number. Verify the license is active, covers the specific work type (plumbing vs. general contractor are different), and is issued in the right jurisdiction (state vs. county vs. city).

What insurance should a contractor have?

At minimum: General Liability ($1M+ per occurrence) and Workers' Compensation (if they have employees). Some trades also carry professional liability or errors and omissions coverage. Ask for a COI (Certificate of Insurance) and verify by calling the insurer directly using the number on the certificate.

Should I pay a contractor upfront?

No more than 10-30% upfront as a deposit to cover initial materials. Final payment (at least 10-15%) should be held until all work is complete and any punch list items are resolved. Never pay in full before work begins -- legitimate contractors don't require this.

How many estimates should I get?

At least 3 written estimates for any job over $1,000. This establishes a market-rate baseline and helps you identify outliers in either direction. Get all estimates to scope the same exact work for a fair comparison.

What if I'm unhappy with completed work?

First, document your concerns in writing and communicate them to the contractor. Reference your contract's scope and warranty terms. Give them a reasonable opportunity to remedy the issue. If they refuse, your options include: (1) withholding final payment, (2) filing a complaint with the state licensing board, (3) filing a BBB complaint, (4) small claims court for smaller amounts, (5) hiring a construction attorney for large disputes.
