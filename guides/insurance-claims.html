The Homeowner's Complete Guide to Insurance Claims

How to file, document, and maximize your claim for water damage, fire, mold, storm, and other covered home disasters.

Updated April 2026 15 min read By HomeServiceDirectory Editorial Team

When disaster strikes your home -- a burst pipe flooding the basement, a kitchen fire, storm damage to your roof -- the insurance claim process can feel overwhelming. This guide walks you through every step, from the moment damage occurs to receiving your final settlement check.

First priority: Safety first. Before thinking about insurance, ensure everyone is safe and the property is secure. Turn off water, gas, or electricity as needed. Only document and begin cleanup once it's safe.

What Does Homeowners Insurance Cover?

Standard homeowners insurance (HO-3 policy) typically covers dwelling damage from sudden and accidental events. Understanding what's covered -- and what isn't -- is critical before you file.

Type of Damage Typically Covered? Notes
Burst pipe / appliance overflow Yes Sudden & accidental water damage
Roof damage from storm/hail Yes Wind/hail perils covered on most policies
Fire and smoke damage Yes Including accidental kitchen fires
Lightning strike damage Yes Structural and electronics
Mold from covered water event Sometimes Usually sublimit of $5K-$10K
Flooding from outside No Requires separate NFIP or private flood policy
Gradual leak / slow water damage No Considered lack of maintenance
Earthquake No Requires separate earthquake policy
Mold from flooding / neglect No Not covered without covered trigger event
Foundation settlement / cracking No Usually excluded as earth movement

Immediate Steps After Damage Occurs

  1. Ensure everyone is safe Evacuate if necessary. Call 911 for fires or structural emergencies. Turn off water at the main shutoff for burst pipes.
  2. Prevent further damage (mitigation) Your policy requires you to take reasonable steps to prevent additional damage. Tarp a damaged roof, board up broken windows, extract standing water. Save all receipts for emergency repairs.
  3. Document everything before cleanup Take extensive photos and video of ALL damage -- every room, every angle. Capture serial numbers on damaged appliances. Do this BEFORE any cleanup or repair begins.
  4. Contact your insurance company Call or use the mobile app to report the claim. Most insurers have 24/7 claims hotlines. Get a claim number. Ask about emergency living expenses (ALE) if your home is uninhabitable.
  5. Get a licensed contractor's written estimate Find licensed, insured contractors through HomeServiceDirectory. Get at least 2-3 written estimates to understand the true scope of damage.
  6. Meet with the adjuster The insurance company sends an adjuster to assess damage. Be present, walk them through everything, and provide your documentation. Don't sign any settlement releases until you're satisfied.

Filing a Water Damage Claim

Water damage is the most common home insurance claim, averaging $11,000 per incident. Here's what you need to know:

What's typically covered

  • Burst or frozen pipes
  • Accidental discharge from appliances (washing machine, dishwasher, water heater)
  • Overflow from toilet, bathtub, or sink
  • Ice dam damage to roof/interior
  • Sudden roof collapse from weight of snow

What's typically NOT covered

  • Flood damage from outside the home (rising water, storm surge)
  • Gradual leaks (slow pipe drip, long-term roof leak)
  • Sump pump failure (unless you added this endorsement)
  • Sewer/drain backup (unless you added this endorsement)
  • Damage from lack of maintenance
Pro Tip: Add a sewer backup and sump pump failure endorsement to your policy. It typically costs $30-$70/year and can cover tens of thousands in damage that standard policies exclude.

Documentation checklist for water damage claims

  • Photos/video of standing water, wet materials, damaged items
  • Photo of the failed component (broken pipe, cracked fitting)
  • Plumber's report confirming the cause and date of failure
  • Moisture readings from a certified water damage restoration company
  • Inventory of all damaged personal property with estimated values

Filing a Fire Damage Claim

Fire claims are typically among the most comprehensive -- most standard policies cover structural damage, personal property loss, additional living expenses, and debris removal.

Key coverage components

  • Dwelling coverage (Coverage A): Repairs to the structure of your home
  • Personal property (Coverage C): Replacement of belongings -- furniture, electronics, clothing
  • Additional Living Expenses (ALE/Coverage D): Hotel, meals, rental car while your home is being restored
  • Smoke and soot damage: Typically included -- can affect every room even if fire was contained
Avoid these common fire claim mistakes: Don't begin cleanup or throw anything away before documenting. Don't sign a Quick Settlement Release without fully understanding the scope of damage. Don't let contractors begin work before getting approval from your insurer.

Smoke damage documentation

Smoke damage can permeate into HVAC systems, insulation, walls, and personal belongings throughout the home -- not just the room where the fire occurred. Document every room and have a certified fire restoration company assess the full scope.

Filing a Mold Remediation Claim

Mold coverage is one of the most disputed areas of homeowners insurance. The key factor is the cause of the mold.

Mold covered by insurance

  • Mold that resulted directly from a covered water damage event (burst pipe, storm)
  • Mold discovered within a reasonable time after the covered event

Mold NOT covered by insurance

  • Mold from flooding (no flood policy)
  • Mold from long-term slow leaks or humidity issues
  • Mold in unfinished basements/crawl spaces from chronic moisture
  • Mold exceeding your policy's sublimit (often $5K-$10K)
Get an independent mold inspector first. A certified industrial hygienist (CIH) can document the extent of contamination and identify the source -- critical for proving the mold is from a covered event. Find certified mold remediators at HomeServiceDirectory.

Filing a Storm Damage Claim

Hail, wind, and storm damage claims spike dramatically after major weather events. Insurance companies become backlogged, so filing promptly and documenting thoroughly is especially important.

Tips for storm damage claims

  • Document damage immediately -- before temporary repairs, before further weather degrades evidence
  • Note the date and approximate time of the storm event
  • Pull storm reports from Weather Underground or the National Weather Service to confirm the storm
  • Get a licensed roofing contractor to inspect for hail and wind damage -- they know how to identify covered damage
  • Be cautious of storm-chasing contractors who appear door-to-door; verify licenses through your state contractor board

Working with the Insurance Adjuster

The adjuster works for the insurance company, not for you. Understanding this relationship helps you advocate for a fair settlement.

Your rights

  • You can hire a public adjuster to advocate on your behalf (typically 10-15% of settlement)
  • You can dispute the adjuster's estimate by providing contractor estimates
  • You can invoke the appraisal clause in your policy if you can't agree on value
  • You can file a complaint with your state insurance commissioner

Red flags in a settlement offer

  • Settlement offered before full scope of damage is known
  • Adjuster discounts contractor estimates without explanation
  • Pressure to sign a quick settlement release
  • Depreciation applied to items that shouldn't be depreciated

Typical Claim Timeline

Understanding the process helps set expectations:

  • Day 1: File claim, get claim number
  • Days 2-5: Adjuster assigned and contacts you
  • Days 5-10: Adjuster inspects property
  • Days 10-21: Initial estimate/coverage determination issued
  • Days 21-45: Negotiate scope, get contractor estimates, dispute underpayment
  • Days 45-90: Repairs begin (with insurer approval)
  • Days 90-180: Recoverable depreciation released upon repair completion
RCV vs. ACV: Know the difference. Replacement Cost Value (RCV) pays to replace damaged items at today's prices. Actual Cash Value (ACV) pays replacement cost minus depreciation. RCV policies cost more but pay significantly more when you have a claim.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will filing a claim raise my insurance rates?

Possibly. Claims history can affect your rates at renewal, especially if you file multiple claims within a few years. Most insurers look back 3-5 years. For very small claims (under your deductible or slightly above), paying out of pocket may preserve your claims-free discount. However, for large claims, use your insurance -- that's what it's for.

What if my claim is denied?

Request the denial in writing with specific policy language cited. Review your policy to understand the exclusion. You can: (1) provide additional documentation to appeal, (2) hire a public adjuster, (3) consult a policyholder attorney, (4) file a complaint with your state insurance commissioner. Many denials are successfully reversed on appeal.

Can I do the repairs myself and keep the insurance money?

For personal property, generally yes. For dwelling repairs, insurers typically require that work be done and RCV only releases depreciation holdback upon completion. Doing substandard DIY repairs may also void coverage for related future claims. Consult your policy and adjuster before attempting DIY on structural repairs.

Do I need a public adjuster?

For large or complex claims (over $10,000-$15,000), a public adjuster often pays for themselves. Studies show public adjusters typically negotiate 20-50% higher settlements. For smaller straightforward claims, a PA may not be necessary. Most PAs work on contingency (percentage of settlement), so there's no upfront cost.
